  • Working Conditions and Health over the Life Cycle: Evidence from Europe
    With Michele Belloni and Claudio Lucifora
    Selected presentations: Netspar International Pension Workshop, Leiden (2026); EuHEA Conference, Rotterdam (2026); Colloquia on Health and Education, Collegio Carlo Alberto, Turin (2026); CPHEB–EEF Seminar, University of Groningen (2026); iHEA World Congress, Bali (2025); EuHEA PhD–Supervisor Conference, UCLouvain, Brussels (2025); Age-IT–OECD Silver Economy Workshop, OECD, Paris (2025); Second Age-IT General Meeting, Naples (2025)
    This study investigates the causal effect of career-long exposure to harmful working conditions on later-life physical and mental health among older Europeans. We combine life-cycle job histories from SHARE data with harmonized historical measures of occupational strain from DOT and O*NET to construct exposure to physical job demands between 1965 and 2017. Identification relies on a Bartik-type shift-share instrument combining US task-routinization trends with pre-determined indicators of job arduousness measured before workers entered the labor market. This approach addresses sorting and reverse causation that bias OLS estimates. Results show clear gender patterns: long-run physical strain worsens men's physical health and women's mental and physical health, while mental-health effects for men are absent. These findings remain robust after accounting for other dimensions of working conditions. Findings highlight the need for policies aimed at improving job quality and addressing gender-specific occupational health disparities in aging Europe.
  • Does It Take a Village to Raise a Child? Parental Health Shocks and Fertility Timing in the Netherlands
    With Viola Angelini and Max Groneck
    Selected presentations: Netspar Pension Day, Utrecht (2026); AIES Annual Conference, Milan (2026)
    This paper studies how parental health shocks affect the fertility of adult children. Using population-wide administrative data from the Netherlands for 2005–2019, we compare sons and daughters whose parent dies or is hospitalized for the first time with sons and daughters whose parent experiences the same type of shock five years later, in a dynamic difference-in-differences design. A parental death lowers the probability that daughters have a child in the following year by 4 percent of the sample mean, with a larger but less precise estimate after a sudden death (8.5 percent). Births recover from the second year. For sons, the estimates after a death are small and not significant. A first parental hospitalization lowers the annual probability of a birth by about 3.5 percent of the sample mean for both sons and daughters, and the effect does not recover over the five years after the shock. First births account for most of the decline, and among daughters the effect is more than twice as large when the mother is hospitalized. The more persistent decline after a hospitalization is consistent with a role for the care needs of a sick parent, in addition to the loss of support.
