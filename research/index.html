  • Working Conditions and Health at Work Over the Life Cycle: Evidence from Europe
    With Michele Belloni and Claudio Lucifora
    Presentations: PhD Workshop, Università Cattolica, Milan (2026); Netspar International Pension Workshop, Leiden (2026); EuHEA Conference, Rotterdam (2026); Colloquia on Health and Education, Collegio Carlo Alberto, Turin (2026); CPHEB–EEF Seminar, University of Groningen (2026); iHEA World Congress, Bali (2025); EuHEA PhD–Supervisor Conference, UCLouvain, Brussels (2025); Age-IT–OECD Silver Economy Workshop, OECD, Paris (2025); Second Age-IT General Meeting, Naples (2025); DEFAP PhD Seminar, Università Cattolica, Milan (2025)
    The study examines how cumulative exposure to adverse working conditions affects later-life physical and mental health among Europeans. Using longitudinal data from SHARE linked to historical occupational measures from O*NET and the 1965 DOT, it applies an instrumental-variable approach based on Bartik-type variation to address endogeneity and measurement error. Health is modeled as a durable capital stock that depreciates with age and harmful work environments. The analysis constructs long-term indices of physical and psychosocial strain and reveals gender-differentiated effects, emphasizing the lasting impact of working conditions on health and the importance of improving job quality for an aging workforce.
  • Does It Take a Village to Raise a Child? Parental Health Shocks and Fertility Timing in the Netherlands
    With Viola Angelini and Max Groneck
    Presentations: Netspar Pension Day, Utrecht (2026); AIES Annual Conference, Milan (2026)
    This paper studies how parental health shocks affect the fertility of adult children. Using population-wide administrative data from the Netherlands for 2005–2019, we compare sons and daughters whose parent dies or is hospitalized for the first time with sons and daughters whose parent experiences the same type of shock five years later, in a dynamic difference-in-differences design. A parental death lowers the probability that daughters have a child in the following year by 4 percent of the sample mean, with a larger but less precise estimate after a sudden death (8.5 percent). Births recover from the second year. For sons, the estimates after a death are small and not significant. A first parental hospitalization lowers the annual probability of a birth by about 3.5 percent of the sample mean for both sons and daughters, and the effect does not recover over the five years after the shock. First births account for most of the decline, and among daughters the effect is more than twice as large when the mother is hospitalized. The more persistent decline after a hospitalization is consistent with a role for the care needs of a sick parent, in addition to the loss of support.
